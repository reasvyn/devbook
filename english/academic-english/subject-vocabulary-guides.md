### How to Use These Guides

When you encounter an unfamiliar term while reading a DevBook subject, follow this lookup procedure:

1. **Identify the subject area** — determine whether the term belongs to philosophy, psychology, mathematics, computer science theory, linguistics, or network/security. If it is a general academic word (e.g., "analysis," "context," "paradigm"), use [Academic Vocabulary](academic-vocabulary.md) instead.
2. **Check this guide first** — if the term belongs to one of the six subject areas below, its definition and example are here.
3. **If not found here**, consult [Academic Vocabulary](academic-vocabulary.md) — it covers 700+ general academic words and additional discipline-specific terms for philosophy, psychology, mathematics, and computer science.
4. **If still not found**, the term may be defined inline in the document you are reading — academic prose typically defines specialized terms on first use, often in parentheses or after a dash.
5. **For terms outside these six areas**, use an external dictionary (Merriam-Webster, OED) or the referenced subject's own glossary in its `intro/` directory.

Each table below follows the same format: the word, a part-of-speech label, a one-line definition, and a subject-relevant example sentence. The definitions are tailored to how the term appears in DevBook — they reflect the academic usage within each discipline rather than everyday usage.

#### Why Subject-Specific Definitions Matter

Many words shift meaning across disciplines. A "theorem" in mathematics is a proved proposition; in everyday speech it can mean any established principle. "Semantics" in linguistics is the study of meaning; in computing it refers to the behavior of code. Reading academic prose requires knowing which sense of a word is active in a given context. These guides provide that contextual signal by anchoring each definition to its discipline.

#### Building a Lookup Habit

The most effective approach is to treat vocabulary building as a byproduct of reading, not a separate activity. When you open a DevBook subject for the first time, bookmark or open this file alongside it. Every time you stop on a word you cannot define precisely, search this page. The search-and-find cycle takes seconds and reinforces the word-meaning connection more effectively than pre-reading vocabulary lists.

Over time, repeated exposure across subjects will consolidate these terms into your active academic vocabulary. A word like "ontology" will appear in philosophy (the study of being), then in computer science (knowledge representation), then in mathematics (formal ontologies). Each encounter deepens and refines your understanding.

#### A Note on Part-of-Speech Labels

The part-of-speech (POS) labels in the tables below use standard abbreviations: n. for noun, v. for verb, adj. for adjective, adv. for adverb. Some words function as multiple parts of speech across contexts — "abstract" can be an adjective (abstract concept), a noun (in the abstract), or a verb (to abstract away details). The POS label corresponds to the most common academic usage of the word within its subject area.

## Learning Tips

Use these guides as a just-in-time reference rather than a study list. When reading a DevBook subject, keep this document open in a split pane. When you encounter a word you do not recognize, search this page first. Repeated lookup strengthens recall through spaced repetition — the same word will cross your path in different subjects and contexts.

### Strategy 1: Contextual Lookup

When you encounter an unfamiliar word while reading, do not reach for a dictionary immediately. Try to infer the meaning from context first, then check your inference against the definition in this guide. This prediction-verification cycle strengthens neural pathways more than passive lookup. If your inference was correct, the word will be more memorable. If incorrect, the correction creates a strong encoding event.

### Strategy 2: Subject-of-the-Week

For deliberate study, pick one subject per week. Read through its vocabulary table once daily for five consecutive days. After each reading session, write one original sentence for each word — a sentence about a topic that interests you, not a copy of the example. This forces your brain to encode the word in a personally meaningful context. By the end of the week, the specialized vocabulary will shift from passive recognition to active recall.

### Strategy 3: Cross-Subject Connection

Many academic terms appear across multiple DevBook subjects with related but distinct meanings. When you encounter a word in a second subject, compare its definition in the new context with its definition in the original context. This comparative encoding produces deeper understanding than isolated study. Examples of fruitful cross-subject terms include:

- **Schema** — appears in psychology (mental frameworks) and computer science (database structure)
- **Abstraction** — appears in philosophy (mental separation of properties) and computer science (information hiding)
- **Syntax** — appears in linguistics (sentence structure) and computer science (grammar rules)
- **Semantics** — appears in linguistics (meaning) and computer science (program behavior)
- **Protocol** — appears in philosophy (ethical conduct) and networking (communication rules)

### Strategy 4: Spaced Repetition Integration

If you use a spaced repetition system (Anki, SuperMemo, or similar), convert the vocabulary tables into flashcards. Each card should contain the word on the front and the definition plus your own example sentence on the back. The examples in this guide serve as a starting point, but cards are more effective when you write your own examples based on material you have recently read.

### Common Pitfalls

- **Learning in isolation.** Studying vocabulary lists without reading actual content produces shallow knowledge that does not transfer to real comprehension. Always pair vocabulary study with active reading.
- **Over-relying on translation.** If English is not your first language, resist the urge to mentally translate each word into your native language. Aim to think the definition in English directly. This builds fluency faster.
- **Treating all words equally.** Academic terms vary in frequency and importance. Words like "hypothesis" and "theory" appear in nearly every subject. Words like "sociolect" or "non-repudiation" appear only in specific contexts. Prioritize high-frequency terms first.
- **Skipping the example sentence.** The definition alone is often insufficient for understanding how a word functions in academic prose. The example sentence shows syntactic behavior — what prepositions follow the word, what grammatical constructions it participates in.
