# Learning Pyramid and Active Learning — Intermediate

## Description

This document provides a deeper analysis of the learning pyramid myth, examining the provenance research in detail, the psychological mechanisms that sustain citation cascades in education, and the genuine evidence base for multimedia learning principles and generative learning strategies. The goal is to equip developers with the ability to distinguish evidence-based claims from unsupported assertions when evaluating learning advice.

The ability to evaluate educational claims is particularly important for developers, who are constantly bombarded with learning advice — "learn by doing," "never stop coding," "watch tutorials," "don't watch tutorials." Much of this advice is well-intentioned but poorly supported. This document provides the tools to evaluate such advice critically, using the learning pyramid myth as a case study in how pseudoscientific claims persist in education.

The document is structured in three parts: first, a detailed provenance analysis of the learning pyramid myth; second, an examination of the genuine evidence base for multimedia and generative learning; and third, practical guidance for designing multimodal learning experiences for developers.

## The Provenance Analysis in Detail

Provenance analysis — the systematic tracing of a claim back to its original source — is a critical skill for evaluating educational research. Most educational claims are never subjected to this level of scrutiny, which is precisely why myths like the learning pyramid persist. This section examines the methodology and findings of the definitive provenance analyses conducted by Masters and Subramony et al.

### Masters (2013): Tracing the Numbers

Ken Masters published "Edgar Dale's Pyramid of Learning in Medical Education" in *Medical Teacher* (2013), conducting the most thorough provenance analysis of the learning pyramid. His investigation traced the claim through decades of citations:

**1946** — Dale publishes *Audio-Visual Methods in Teaching* with the Cone of Experience. The cone contains 11 levels. No percentages are attached to any level. Dale explicitly warns against overinterpreting the model.

**1954, 1960, 1969** — Dale publishes revised editions of his book. The cone remains unchanged in its essential structure. No percentages appear in any edition.

**Approximately 1970** — An unknown source superimposes retention percentages onto a simplified version of Dale's cone. The specific numbers (10/20/30/50/70/90%) appear for the first time. This is the pivotal moment — the point at which a descriptive model becomes a fabricated quantitative claim.

**1970s-2000s** — The pyramid with percentages circulates in training materials, textbooks, and educational presentations. It is increasingly attributed to Dale and/or the National Training Laboratories (NTL).

**2013** — Masters publishes his analysis, concluding: "The Pyramid is rubbish, the statistics are rubbish, and they do not come from Edgar Dale."

Masters's methodology is instructive for anyone interested in evaluating educational claims:

1. **Database search** — he searched major academic databases for all citations of the learning pyramid, identifying every peer-reviewed article that referenced the model.
2. **Source tracing** — for each citation, he traced the reference chain backward, looking for the primary source. In every case, the chain led to an earlier citation that also lacked a primary source.
3. **Institutional consultation** — he contacted the NTL directly, requesting the original research data that supposedly supported the percentages. The NTL could not produce it.
4. **Edition review** — he reviewed every edition of Dale's book, confirming that no percentages appeared in any version.
5. **Visual comparison** — he compared the pyramid diagrams across different sources, documenting how the simplified version with percentages differed from Dale's original cone.

This kind of systematic provenance analysis is rare in educational research. Most claims that circulate in education are never subjected to this level of scrutiny — which is precisely why myths like the learning pyramid persist.

The methodology is transferable. When you encounter a learning claim — in a textbook, a blog post, a conference talk, or a training program — you can apply the same steps: search for the primary source, trace the citation chain backward, check whether the original research actually supports the claim, and look for independent replications. This is the same basic critical thinking process that developers apply to technical claims, applied to educational claims.

The learning pyramid is a particularly clear case because the provenance analysis has been published and peer-reviewed. In most cases, you would need to conduct the analysis yourself — but the skills are the same: systematic searching, source tracing, and evidence evaluation.

### The NTL Attribution

The National Training Laboratories in Bethel, Maine, is frequently cited as the source of the retention percentages. When researchers attempted to verify this attribution:

- The NTL was unable to locate or produce any original research supporting the percentages.
- The NTL acknowledged that Dale produced "a similar pyramid with slightly different numbers" — but Dale's actual cone contains no numbers.
- No NTL publication from the relevant period contains the data that would support the claimed percentages.

The NTL attribution is particularly instructive because it illustrates how institutional credibility functions as a cognitive shortcut. The NTL was (and is) a legitimate and respected training organization. The mere fact that a reputable institution is associated with a claim is sufficient for most readers to accept the claim without verification. The NTL's inability to produce the research that supposedly supports the percentages is a detail that most citers never discover — because most citers never investigate the source.

This pattern — attribution to a credible institution, followed by widespread acceptance without verification — is not unique to the learning pyramid. It is a common mechanism in the propagation of pseudoscientific claims in education and beyond.

### Subramony et al. (2014)

Subramony, Molenda, Betrus, and Thalheimer confirmed Masters's findings through independent analysis. They traced the superimposition of numbers onto Dale's cone to an unknown source around 1970 and documented the absence of any supporting empirical research.

The importance of independent confirmation cannot be overstated. In science, a single study — no matter how thorough — is not sufficient to establish a finding. The fact that Masters's analysis was independently replicated by a separate research team, using different methods, reaching identical conclusions, provides strong evidence that the debunking is correct. This is how scientific knowledge works: not through individual authority, but through convergent evidence from independent sources.

## Citation Cascade: How Myths Survive in Academia

The learning pyramid is a case study in citation cascade — a phenomenon where a claim gains perceived credibility through repetition rather than evidence. The mechanism operates through several stages:

### Stage 1: Original Claim

A claim is made in a specific context with appropriate qualifications and limitations. Dale's cone was a descriptive model of experience types, explicitly presented as non-hierarchical and non-quantitative. The qualifications were clear — and they were lost.

### Stage 2: Simplification and Decontextualization

The claim is simplified and stripped of its original qualifications. Dale's 11-level cone becomes a 6-level pyramid. The cautionary notes are removed. Qualifiers like "approximately" and "in some contexts" disappear. This simplification makes the claim easier to share and remember, but it also makes it inaccurate.

The simplification process is driven by cognitive economy: the simplified version is easier to process and remember than the original. This connects to the fluency heuristic — information that is easy to process is judged as more true and more important.

### Stage 3: Attribution to Authority

The simplified claim is attributed to a respected source. The pyramid is attributed to Dale (a legitimate educational researcher) or NTL (a legitimate institution). This attribution provides credibility without verification.

The authority attribution exploits a well-documented cognitive bias: people are more likely to accept claims from perceived authorities. This heuristic is usually reliable — most claims attributed to respected researchers are, in fact, supported by their work. The learning pyramid exploits this heuristic by attaching a legitimate name to an illegitimate claim.

### Stage 4: Textbook Inclusion

The claim appears in textbooks. Textbook authors often rely on other textbooks rather than primary sources. Once the pyramid appears in one textbook, subsequent authors cite it as established knowledge.

Textbook inclusion is a critical stage because textbooks are perceived as authoritative and reliable. Students and educators who encounter the pyramid in a textbook assume it has been fact-checked and verified. In reality, textbook authors face tight deadlines, limited budgets, and enormous scope — fact-checking every claim against primary sources is impractical.

### Stage 5: Self-Reinforcing Citation

Each citation reinforces the perception that the claim is well-established. The volume of citations creates an illusion of independent corroboration — but all citations trace back to the same unsubstantiated source.

This stage is self-reinforcing because citation metrics (h-index, citation count) are used to evaluate research quality. A claim that has been cited 43 times appears more credible than one cited 3 times, regardless of whether those citations are independent verifications or merely repetitions.

### Stage 6: Resistance to Correction

Even after debunking (Masters, 2013; Subramony et al., 2014), the pyramid persists because:

- It appears in established textbooks that are not updated frequently.
- It is convenient for advocates of active learning (it supports their position).
- It is visually compelling and easy to remember.
- The correction is less interesting than the original claim.
- The people who cite the pyramid are typically not the people who read debunking articles.

The persistence of the pyramid illustrates the "continued influence effect" — the tendency for misinformation to continue to influence reasoning even after it has been corrected. This is one of the most robust findings in the misinformation literature.

The correction challenge is compounded by the fact that corrections are less memorable than the original claim. The pyramid image is vivid, memorable, and easy to share. The correction is detailed, nuanced, and requires understanding of provenance analysis. In a competition between simplicity and accuracy, simplicity often wins — especially in the fast-paced, low-attention environment of social media and professional development.

### The 43-Article Problem

Masters documented that the learning pyramid had been cited in at least 43 peer-reviewed medical education articles. In each case, the citation was presented as established fact, not as an empirical claim requiring verification. This illustrates a systemic failure in academic citation practices.

The 43 articles represent a failure at multiple levels:

1. **Author failure** — 43 sets of authors cited the pyramid without verifying its source.
2. **Reviewer failure** — peer reviewers did not flag the unsupported claim.
3. **Editor failure** — journal editors did not question the citation.
4. **Reader failure** — subsequent researchers who read these articles cited the pyramid in their own work without questioning it.

This cascade of failures is not unique to the learning pyramid. It is a systemic vulnerability in academic publishing that affects claims across many fields.

## Mayer's Multimedia Learning Principles in Depth

Richard Mayer's multimedia learning principles represent what the evidence actually supports about learning modalities — as opposed to the fabricated percentages of the learning pyramid.

### The Twelve Principles (Mayer, 2009, 2021)

Mayer's twelve multimedia learning principles represent the most comprehensive evidence-based framework for instructional design. Each principle is supported by multiple experimental studies and has been replicated across diverse populations and materials:

| # | Principle | Statement | Evidence |
|---|-----------|-----------|----------|
| 1 | Multimedia | People learn better from words and pictures together | Strong |
| 2 | Spatial contiguity | Place text near corresponding graphics | Strong |
| 3 | Temporal contiguity | Present narration and animation simultaneously | Strong |
| 4 | Coherence | Exclude extraneous words, pictures, and sounds | Strong |
| 5 | Modality | Use narration with graphics rather than text with graphics | Strong |
| 6 | Redundancy | Do not add on-screen text to narration and graphics | Strong |
| 7 | Signaling | Highlight essential material | Moderate |
| 8 | Segmenting | Present in learner-paced segments | Moderate |
| 9 | Pre-training | Teach key concepts before the main lesson | Moderate |
| 10 | Personalization | Use conversational rather than formal style | Moderate |
| 11 | Voice | Use human voice rather than machine voice | Moderate |
| 12 | Image | No evidence that adding images of the speaker helps | Weak |

The principles are organized by evidence strength: principles 1–6 have strong evidence (replicated across many studies, large effect sizes); principles 7–10 have moderate evidence (replicated in some studies, moderate effect sizes); principles 11–12 have weak or mixed evidence. This hierarchy is useful for prioritizing which principles to apply when resources are limited.

### What These Principles Replace

The multimedia principles replace the learning pyramid's false quantitative claims with genuine, evidence-based qualitative principles. They do not tell you what percentage of information is retained through different modalities. They tell you that combining words and pictures works better than words alone, and that specific design choices (contiguity, coherence, modality) improve learning outcomes.

This is a more useful framework than the pyramid because it provides actionable design guidance. Rather than knowing that "doing is 75% effective" (a meaningless claim), you learn that placing explanatory text directly on diagrams (spatial contiguity) improves comprehension compared to placing text below the diagram. This is specific, testable, and practically useful.

The multimedia principles also have a boundary condition that the learning pyramid ignores: cognitive load. Each principle operates within the constraint of limited working memory. When cognitive load is high (complex material, novice learners), the principles are most important — and violations of the principles are most costly. When cognitive load is low (simple material, expert learners), the principles still apply but their effects are smaller.

### Evidence Strength by Principle

The principles vary in the strength of their evidence base:

**Strong evidence** (replicated across multiple studies, large effect sizes):
- Multimedia principle (d = 1.0–1.5)
- Spatial contiguity (d = 0.8–1.2)
- Temporal contiguity (d = 0.7–1.0)
- Coherence (d = 0.8–1.3)
- Modality (d = 0.7–1.1)

**Moderate evidence** (replicated in some studies, moderate effect sizes):
- Segmenting (d = 0.4–0.7)
- Personalization (d = 0.3–0.6)
- Pre-training (d = 0.4–0.8)
- Signaling (d = 0.3–0.7)

**Weak or mixed evidence**:
- Image principle (adding a speaker image does not improve learning)
- Voice principle (human voice better than machine voice — limited evidence)
- Expertise reversal effect (some principles reverse for expert learners)

These effect sizes represent the improvement in learning outcomes when the principle is followed compared to when it is violated. A d of 1.0 means the average learner in the principle-following condition performed better than 84% of learners in the principle-violating condition — a substantial and practically significant difference.

The expertise reversal effect deserves special attention for developers. As expertise in a domain increases, some principles become less effective or even reverse. Expert learners often benefit more from simplified presentations than from multimedia presentations, because the multimedia elements may be redundant with their existing knowledge. This means that the multimedia principles are most important for beginners and intermediate learners, while experts may benefit from more streamlined presentations.

### Application to Developer Documentation

| Principle | Documentation Application | Anti-Pattern |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------|
| Multimedia | Combine text explanations with code examples and diagrams | Text-only API reference |
| Spatial contiguity | Place parameter descriptions directly in code examples | Separating code and explanation into different sections |
| Coherence | Remove decorative elements, unnecessary screenshots | Adding company branding to every code example |
| Modality | Use voiceover in video tutorials rather than text overlays | Subtitles that duplicate the narration |
| Segmenting | Break long tutorials into short, focused sections | Single continuous tutorial with no navigation |
| Pre-training | Define key terms before using them in procedures | Assuming readers know all terminology |

The spatial contiguity principle is particularly underutilized in developer documentation. Many documentation sites place code examples in one section and explanations in another, violating the principle. A simple improvement: add inline comments to code examples, placing the explanation directly adjacent to the code it describes. This typically produces a 20–40% improvement in comprehension (Mayer, 2009).

## Noetel et al. (2022): The Meta-Meta-Analysis

Noetel and colleagues conducted a comprehensive meta-meta-analysis published in *Review of Educational Research*, synthesizing evidence from hundreds of meta-analyses on learning techniques. This study is the most comprehensive evidence synthesis available on learning strategies, and its conclusions should be the primary reference point for anyone evaluating educational claims.

The meta-meta-analytic approach is particularly valuable because it aggregates findings across diverse populations, materials, and settings — providing a more robust estimate of effect sizes than any individual study or meta-analysis. Key findings:

### Techniques With Strong Evidence

- **Multimedia learning** — combining visual and verbal information consistently improves outcomes. The evidence base spans decades of research across laboratory and classroom settings.
- **Spacing** — distributed practice outperforms massed practice across all conditions tested. This is among the most robust findings in all of psychology.
- **Retrieval practice** — testing produces better long-term retention than restudying. The effect is robust across materials, populations, and retention intervals.
- **Interleaving** — mixing problem types improves discrimination and transfer. The evidence is strongest for mathematical problem-solving and extends to other domains.

### Techniques With Moderate Evidence

- **Elaborative interrogation** — asking "why?" improves learning in many but not all contexts. The effectiveness depends on the learner's ability to generate plausible explanations.
- **Self-explanation** — explaining to yourself during study improves outcomes. The effect is larger when explanations are generated spontaneously rather than prompted.
- **Concrete examples** — connecting abstract concepts to specific instances aids understanding. This is particularly effective for learners with limited prior knowledge.

### Techniques With Weak or No Evidence

- **Learning styles matching** — no support for the claim that matching instruction to style improves outcomes. This is one of the most persistent myths in education, analogous to the learning pyramid in its persistence despite lack of evidence.
- **Unelaborated practice testing without feedback** — testing is most effective when feedback is provided. Without feedback, testing can reinforce incorrect responses.
- **Summarization** — effective when done well, but quality varies enormously. Most learners have not been taught to summarize effectively.

### The Implications for Developers

The Noetel et al. findings provide an evidence-based framework for choosing learning strategies:

1. **Prioritize strategies with strong evidence** — spacing, retrieval practice, interleaving, and multimedia learning are the highest-leverage strategies for developer learning.
2. **Supplement with moderate-evidence strategies** — elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and concrete examples are valuable additions.
3. **Avoid strategies with weak or no evidence** — learning styles matching, unelaborated practice testing, and poor summarization are low-leverage activities.
4. **Combine strategies** — the evidence supports combining multiple techniques rather than relying on any single one. A study routine that combines spacing + retrieval practice + interleaving is more effective than any technique alone.

The practical implication is that developer learning should be designed around the strongest evidence. Instead of spending hours reading documentation (passive reception), developers should spend shorter periods reading followed by active retrieval, self-testing, and spaced review. The total time invested may be less, but the learning outcomes will be substantially better.

## The Generative Learning Framework

Fiorella and Mayer (2015) proposed the generative learning framework — a unifying account of why active learning strategies work. The framework identifies five strategies that require learners to actively generate knowledge.

The generative learning framework is important because it provides a unified theoretical account of why different active learning strategies work. Before this framework, strategies like self-testing, self-explanation, and drawing were treated as separate phenomena with separate explanations. Fiorella and Mayer showed that they share a common mechanism: generation. This theoretical unification is valuable because it allows learners to understand *why* they are doing what they are doing — and to predict which new strategies might also be effective.

The framework also explains the connection between active learning and the multimedia principles: both are grounded in the same cognitive architecture (dual channels, limited capacity, active processing). The multimedia principles tell you how to present information effectively; the generative learning framework tells you how to process it effectively. Together, they provide a comprehensive evidence-based approach to learning design.

### The Five Strategies

| Strategy | What It Involves | Mechanism | Example for Developers |
|----------|------------------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Summarizing** | Creating a brief representation of the material | Selecting and organizing essential information | Write a 3-paragraph summary of a new API from memory |
| **Drawing** | Creating a visual representation (diagram, map, chart) | Integrating verbal and visual information | Draw a sequence diagram for a microservices interaction |
| **Explaining** | Generating explanations for how or why something works | Elaborative processing and self-explanation | Write a blog post explaining how React's virtual DOM works |
| **Mapping** | Creating a visual representation of relationships | Organizing information spatially | Create a concept map linking design patterns to use cases |
| **Self-testing** | Attempting to retrieve information from memory | Retrieval practice and metacognitive feedback | Use Anki flashcards to test yourself on API endpoints |

Each strategy engages different cognitive operations, and the combination produces more durable learning than any single strategy. The key insight is that all five strategies require the learner to *generate* something — a summary, a diagram, an explanation, a map, a test response. This generation is the mechanism that produces deeper encoding than passive reception.

### The Common Mechanism

All five strategies share a common mechanism: they require the learner to *generate* a representation of the material rather than passively receiving one. Generation forces selection (what to include), organization (how to structure), and integration (how it connects to existing knowledge). These cognitive operations produce deeper encoding than passive reception.

The generative learning framework explains *why* the learning pyramid's qualitative ordering is correct: teaching others (explaining) is more effective than doing (building), which is more effective than watching (demonstrating), because each level engages more generative processes.

### When Each Strategy Is Most Effective

| Strategy | Best For | Limitations |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| **Summarizing** | Synthesizing long texts or lectures | Quality varies enormously; requires practice |
| **Drawing** | Spatial relationships, system architectures | Requires basic drawing skills; may be slow |
| **Explaining** | Conceptual understanding, identifying gaps | Requires prior knowledge to explain accurately |
| **Mapping** | Relationships between many concepts | Can become cluttered with complex material |
| **Self-testing** | Factual knowledge, procedures | Less effective for conceptual understanding alone |

The most effective approach is to combine multiple strategies. For example, when learning a new technology: read the documentation (passive reception) → draw an architecture diagram (drawing) → explain the architecture to a colleague (explaining) → test yourself on key concepts (self-testing). Each strategy engages different cognitive operations, and the combination produces more durable learning than any single strategy.

### Generative Learning for Developers

| Strategy | Developer Application |
|----------|----------------------|
| Summarizing | After reading documentation, write a 3-paragraph summary of the key concepts from memory |
| Drawing | Create a data flow diagram for a system you are learning about |
| Explaining | Write a blog post or give a tech talk explaining a concept |
| Mapping | Create a concept map linking related APIs, libraries, or design patterns |
| Self-testing | Use Anki or practice problems to retrieve key facts from memory |

The common thread across all these applications is the requirement to *generate* something — a summary, a diagram, an explanation, a map, a test response. Generation is the mechanism. The specific form it takes matters less than the fact that it occurs.

## Designing Multimodal Learning Experiences

### For Self-Study

A multimodal study session for learning a new technology might include:

1. **Read** the documentation (textual processing).
2. **Watch** a conference talk on the topic (visual + auditory processing).
3. **Build** a small project (generative learning through drawing/building).
4. **Explain** the concept to a colleague or rubber duck (generative learning through explaining).
5. **Test yourself** on key concepts (retrieval practice).
6. **Create** a diagram of the system architecture (generative learning through mapping).

Each modality creates different encoding opportunities. The combination is more effective than any single modality.

### The "Study Cycle" for Developers

A practical study cycle based on the evidence:

1. **Pre-training** (10 minutes) — define key terms and concepts before engaging with the main material. This builds the schemas needed for efficient encoding.
2. **Initial exposure** (20 minutes) — read documentation or watch a tutorial. Do not try to understand everything — focus on the main concepts and their relationships.
3. **Generation** (20 minutes) — close the documentation and draw a diagram, write a summary, or build a small example from memory. This is where the generative learning strategies are applied.
4. **Retrieval practice** (15 minutes) — test yourself on the key concepts. Use flashcards, free recall, or practice problems. This is where the testing effect operates.
5. **Feedback and correction** (10 minutes) — check your answers against the source material. Identify and correct any misunderstandings. This is where the metacognitive feedback loop operates.
6. **Spacing** — schedule a review session for tomorrow (brief), next week (moderate), and next month (brief). This is where the spacing effect operates.

This cycle typically takes 75 minutes and is far more effective than the same 75 minutes spent rereading documentation.

The cycle integrates five evidence-based techniques into a single workflow: pre-training (Mayer), generative learning (Fiorella & Mayer), retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke), feedback (meta-analytic finding), and spacing (Cepeda et al.). Each technique addresses a different aspect of the memory system, and their combination produces multiplicative benefits.

### For Team Learning

- **Tech talks** combine presentation (verbal + visual) with Q&A (retrieval + elaboration). The best tech talks end with live coding or hands-on exercises, not just Q&A. When designing a tech talk, apply the multimedia principles: combine slides with code examples, place explanations near the code, and eliminate decorative elements.
- **Code reviews** require both analytical processing (evaluating code) and generative processing (articulating reasoning). Code review comments that explain *why* something should change are more educational than comments that merely state *what* should change. The "why" forces the reviewer to generate an explanation, which engages elaborative processing.
- **Pair programming** combines visual code analysis, verbal discussion, and generative problem-solving. The pair programming format of driver + navigator ensures that both participants are actively engaged, though in different ways. The driver practices generation (writing code); the navigator practices analysis (evaluating design decisions).
- **Learning groups** can implement spaced retrieval practice by having each member teach a concept from previous sessions, forcing retrieval and elaboration. The group format adds social accountability, which improves consistency of practice.
- **Retrospectives** are a form of generative learning for teams: they require teams to analyze past work, identify patterns, and generate improvements. The best retrospectives include individual reflection (self-testing) before group discussion (explaining).

### For Documentation Design

Effective technical documentation should:

- Combine text with diagrams (multimedia principle).
- Place explanatory text near the code it describes (spatial contiguity).
- Remove unnecessary decorative elements (coherence).
- Use voiceover in video tutorials rather than text overlays (modality).
- Break long tutorials into focused sections (segmenting).
- Define key terms before using them in procedures (pre-training).
- Include check-your-understanding questions at the end of each section (retrieval practice).
- Provide progressive exercises that increase in complexity (generative learning).

The most impactful change for most documentation is implementing spatial contiguity. Many documentation sites separate code examples from their explanations, placing code in one section and prose in another. Moving explanatory comments directly into the code examples — or placing descriptions immediately adjacent to the components they describe — typically produces the largest single improvement in comprehension.

## Learning Tips

- When you encounter a learning claim with specific percentages, investigate the source. If the source cannot be traced to primary research, treat the claim with skepticism.
- The most important practical principle from multimedia learning research is the spatial contiguity principle: place explanations near the things they explain. This single change typically produces the largest improvement in learning outcomes.
- Build multimodal study habits deliberately. If your current study routine relies primarily on one modality (reading, watching, or building), add complementary modalities.
- The generative learning framework provides a practical diagnostic: if your study session involves only receiving information (reading, watching), you are not learning as effectively as you could. Add a generative activity (summarizing, drawing, explaining, testing yourself) to each session.
- When designing technical documentation, apply Mayer's principles as a checklist. Each principle provides a specific, testable improvement that can be evaluated against user comprehension metrics.
- The citation cascade analysis provides a transferable skill: whenever you encounter a claim that is "widely known" or "commonly accepted," investigate whether the claim has ever been tested. Common acceptance is not evidence.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Citation cascade | The phenomenon where a claim gains perceived credibility through repeated citation rather than evidence |
| Provenance analysis | Tracing a claim back to its original source to verify its authenticity |
| Generative learning | Active strategies that require learners to generate representations of material rather than passively receiving them |
| Multimedia principle | People learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone |
| Spatial contiguity | Placing explanatory text near the corresponding visual element |
| Coherence | Eliminating extraneous material from instructional presentations |
| Modality | Using narration with graphics rather than text with graphics |
| Signaling | Highlighting essential material to direct attention |
| Pre-training | Teaching key concepts before presenting the main lesson |
| Segmenting | Breaking continuous presentations into learner-paced segments |
| Illusory truth effect | The tendency to believe claims that are repeated, regardless of their truth |
| Continued influence effect | The tendency for misinformation to continue to influence reasoning after correction |
| Expertise reversal effect | The finding that instructional techniques effective for novices may be less effective or counterproductive for experts |
| Effect size (Cohen's d) | A standardized measure of the magnitude of a treatment effect — d = 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium, 0.8 is large |
| Cognitive load | The total amount of mental effort required to process information in working memory |
| Dual channels | Mayer's assumption that humans process visual and auditory information through separate channels |
| Active processing | The assumption that meaningful learning requires learners to actively select, organize, and integrate information |
| Redundancy principle | People learn better from narration and graphics than from narration, text, and graphics combined |
| Fluency heuristic | The tendency to judge information as more true when it is easy to process |
| Generative learning theory | Fiorella and Mayer's framework identifying five strategies that improve learning through active generation |
| Peer review | The process by which scholarly work is evaluated by other experts in the field before publication |
| Confirmation bias | The tendency to search for and favor information that confirms existing beliefs |
