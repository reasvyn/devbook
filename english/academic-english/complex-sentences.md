# Complex Sentences

## Description

Academic English sentences are longer and more structurally layered than conversational or technical English. This document teaches you to parse those sentences — to identify main clauses, subordinate structures, and logical relationships — so that you can extract meaning from even the densest academic prose.

### Why Academic Sentences Are Long

Academic writing prioritizes precision over brevity. Every relationship between ideas must be stated explicitly — causation, condition, concession, contrast, purpose. These relationships require structural markers: conjunctions, relative pronouns, prepositions, and clause boundaries. A single academic sentence often contains what conversational English would spread across five or six sentences.

Consider the difference:

> **Conversational:** The algorithm failed. It was because the data was noisy. We didn't clean it properly.

> **Academic:** The algorithm failed because the data, which had not been properly cleaned, contained sufficient noise to undermine the model's predictive accuracy.

The academic version compresses three conversational sentences into one by using a subordinate clause (because...), a relative clause (which had not been...), and an infinitive of purpose (to undermine...). The brevity is not simpler — it is denser. Each structural element signals a specific logical relationship.

Academic sentences are long for several reasons:

- **They embed clauses** — main ideas carry subordinate ideas inside them, like parentheses that expand indefinitely.
- **They nominalize actions** — verbs become nouns, which then require new verbs to relate them.
- **They qualify claims** — hedging, conditions, and exceptions are woven into the sentence rather than stated separately.
- **They define terms inline** — an unfamiliar term is often followed by its definition in an appositive or relative clause.

A reader who cannot untangle these structures loses the argument even when every individual word is familiar.

### Subordinate Clauses

A subordinate clause (also called a dependent clause) contains a subject and a verb but cannot stand alone as a sentence. It depends on a main clause for its meaning. Subordinate clauses are the most common source of sentence length in academic writing.

**Identifying the main clause:** The main clause is the part of the sentence that can stand alone. Everything else is subordinate. If you remove all subordinate clauses, the remaining sentence should be grammatically complete and express the core claim.

> **Example:** Although the experiment produced no significant results, the researchers concluded that the methodology, because it had controlled for confounding variables, remained valid for future studies.

**Parse:**
- Main clause: *the researchers concluded...remained valid for future studies*
- Subordinate clause 1 (concession): *Although the experiment produced no significant results*
- Subordinate clause 2 (object of conclusion): *that the methodology...remained valid for future studies*
- Subordinate clause 3 (causal, embedded within clause 2): *because it had controlled for confounding variables*

The sentence makes three logical moves: concede the null result, state the conclusion, and explain why the methodology is still valid. Each move is a subordinate clause.

**Common subordinating conjunctions in academic English:**

| Conjunction | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Although, though, even though | Concession | *Although the sample size was small...* |
| Because, since, as | Cause/reason | *Because the model failed to converge...* |
| Whereas, while | Contrast | *Whereas Kant argues for a priori categories...* |
| If, unless, provided that | Condition | *Provided that the assumptions hold...* |
| In order that, so that | Purpose | *In order that the results are reproducible...* |
| After, before, once, until | Time | *Once the data were collected...* |

**Strategy:** When you see a subordinating conjunction, mark the beginning of the subordinate clause. Find its verb. Then find where the clause ends (usually at the next comma or at the end of the sentence). Remove the clause mentally and see what remains — that is the main claim.

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses modify nouns. They begin with relative pronouns: *which, that, who, whom, whose, where, when.* Academic English uses relative clauses to define terms, add context, and specify which instance of a concept is being discussed.

**Restrictive vs. non-restrictive:**

A restrictive relative clause defines which noun is meant. It is essential to the meaning and is not set off by commas.

> The theory **that posits innate knowledge structures** contradicts empiricist accounts.

Without the clause, we do not know which theory is meant. The clause restricts the reference.

A non-restrictive relative clause adds extra information about an already-identified noun. It is set off by commas.

> Kant's transcendental idealism, **which posits innate categories of understanding**, remains influential in contemporary philosophy.

The clause adds information but does not change which theory is identified. The commas signal that the clause could be removed without losing the sentence's core reference.

**Which vs. that:**

In academic English, *that* introduces restrictive clauses only. *Which* can introduce both restrictive and non-restrictive clauses, though many style guides prefer *which* for non-restrictive only.

> The framework **that the study employs** is based on constructivist principles. (restrictive — which framework? The one the study employs.)

> The framework, **which is widely cited**, has been applied in over 200 studies. (non-restrictive — all frameworks are already identified)

**Whom and whose:**

*Whom* is the object form of *who* and is used when the relative pronoun is the object of the clause's verb or a preposition.

> The researcher **whom** the committee interviewed was exceptionally qualified.

*Whose* indicates possession and is used for both people and things in academic English.

> The theory **whose** assumptions we have questioned remains central to the field.

### Conditional Structures

Conditional sentences express that one event or state depends on another. Academic English uses a wider range of conditionals than conversational English, including inverted conditionals that omit *if*.

**Real conditionals (open conditions):**

These describe conditions that may or may not be true. The present tense in the condition clause indicates an open possibility.

> **If** the data satisfy the normality assumption, then a parametric test is appropriate.

**Counterfactual conditionals (unreal conditions):**

These describe conditions known to be false. The past tense (or past perfect) in the condition clause signals unreality.

> **If** the sample had been larger, the effect would have reached statistical significance. (The sample was not larger.)

**Inverted conditionals:**

Academic English often omits *if* and inverts the subject and auxiliary verb. This is a formal structure that signals a high register.

> **Had the experiment been conducted** under controlled conditions, the results would have been more reliable. (= If the experiment had been conducted...)

> **Were it not for** the confounding variable, the treatment effect would be unambiguous. (= If it were not for...)

> **Should the assumptions prove** invalid, the entire model collapses. (= If the assumptions should prove...)

**Other conditional markers:**

| Marker | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Unless | If...not | *Unless the protocol is followed, the results are invalid.* |
| Provided that | On condition that | *Provided that the sample is representative, the conclusions generalize.* |
| As long as | On condition that | *As long as the assumptions hold, the proof is sound.* |
| Whether...or | Alternative conditions | *Whether the hypothesis is confirmed or refuted, the data are informative.* |

### Participial Phrases

Participial phrases begin with a present participle (*-ing*) or past participle (*-ed, -en, -t*) and function as adjectives or adverbials. They are common in academic writing because they compress information that would otherwise require a full clause.

**Present participial phrases (-ing):**

These describe an action simultaneous with the main verb. They often have an implied subject that matches the main clause subject.

> **Drawing on empirical evidence from five studies**, the author argues that the effect is robust.

- Implied subject: the author
- Full clause equivalent: *The author draws on empirical evidence from five studies and argues...*

> **Having established the theoretical framework**, the paper proceeds to test three hypotheses.

- The perfect participle (*having + past participle*) indicates an action completed before the main verb.
- Full clause equivalent: *After the paper has established the theoretical framework, it proceeds to...*

**Past participial phrases (-ed):**

These are passive in meaning. The noun they modify receives the action.

> **Motivated by a desire to reconcile competing theories**, the researcher designed a new experimental paradigm.

- Full clause equivalent: *The researcher was motivated by a desire to reconcile competing theories, and she designed...*

> **Given the limitations of the current study**, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

- This is a absolute construction — it modifies the entire main clause rather than a single noun.

**Dangling participles:**

A participial phrase must logically attach to the subject of the main clause. When it does not, the result is a dangling participle, which creates ambiguity.

> **Walking through the laboratory**, the equipment appeared outdated.

This suggests the equipment was walking. The intended meaning would be: *Walking through the laboratory, the researcher noticed the equipment appeared outdated.*

### Appositives and Parentheticals

Appositives are noun phrases that rename or define an adjacent noun. Parentheticals are interruptions that add commentary, clarification, or attribution. Both are signaled by commas, dashes, or parentheses.

**Appositives:**

> Nominalization, **the process of turning a verb into a noun**, is a defining feature of academic English.

The appositive (*the process...noun*) defines nominalization. It could replace the noun it renames: *The process of turning a verb into a noun is a defining feature...*

> The philosopher **Immanuel Kant** argued that space and time are a priori intuitions.

Here the appositive is a name that specifies which philosopher is meant.

**Parentheticals with dashes:**

Dashes create a stronger break than commas. They signal that the interruption is significant.

> The theory of evolution — **a framework that has been refined over 150 years of empirical research** — remains the foundation of modern biology.

> Three factors — **sample size, effect magnitude, and measurement reliability** — determine the statistical power of a study.

**Parentheticals with parentheses:**

Parentheses signal that the enclosed material is supplementary — a definition, an example, a citation, or a qualification.

> The construct validity of a test (i.e., **the degree to which it measures the intended theoretical construct**) must be established before the test is used for diagnostic purposes.

> Some philosophers (most notably **Hume and Berkeley**) rejected the concept of material substance altogether.

**Attribution parentheticals:**

Academic writing frequently attributes claims to sources inline.

> The postmodern turn, **as Foucault argued**, represents a shift in the relationship between power and knowledge.

> Cognitive dissonance theory — **first proposed by Festinger in 1957** — predicts that individuals experience psychological discomfort when holding contradictory beliefs.

### Nominalization Chains

Nominalization is the transformation of verbs or adjectives into nouns. In academic English, nominalization allows writers to treat actions and qualities as objects that can be examined, compared, and linked. Chains of nominalizations create dense passages that are difficult to parse if the reader does not recognize the underlying verb.

**Simple nominalization:**

| Verb/Adjective | Nominalization |
|---|---|
| analyze | analysis |
| assume | assumption |
| conclude | conclusion |
| define | definition |
| exist | existence |
| hypothesize | hypothesis |
| justify | justification |
| observe | observation |
| precise | precision |
| verify | verification |

**A nominalization chain:**

> The **conceptualization** of knowledge **acquisition** as a process of **construction** rather than **reception** has significant **implications** for instructional **design**.

Parse by tracking the underlying verbs and actors:
- *Conceptualization* ← to conceptualize (someone conceptualizes X as Y)
- *Acquisition* ← to acquire (someone acquires knowledge)
- *Construction* ← to construct (learners construct knowledge)
- *Reception* ← to receive (learners receive knowledge)
- *Implications* ← to imply (X implies Y)
- *Design* ← to design (someone designs instruction)

**Parsing a nominalization chain:**

1. Identify each nominalization and mentally convert it back to a verb.
2. Ask: Who is the implied actor? What is the implied action?
3. Reconstruct the underlying events or relationships.

> The **identification** of causal **relationships** between variables requires the **isolation** of confounding factors through experimental **control**.

- *identification* → identify (researchers identify relationships)
- *relationships* → relate (variables relate to one another)
- *isolation* → isolate (researchers isolate confounding factors)
- *control* → control (researchers control conditions)

The sentence means: *To identify causal relationships between variables, researchers must isolate confounding factors by controlling experimental conditions.*

### Parsing Strategy: Find the Verb

The single most effective strategy for parsing any academic sentence is to find the main verb. Once you have the main verb, you have the core claim. Everything else is elaboration, qualification, or context.

**Step-by-step strategy:**

1. **Find the main verb.** Look for the finite verb (a verb that changes tense and agrees with a subject). Ignore infinitives (*to verb*), participles (*-ing, -ed*), and gerunds (*-ing* as noun). The main verb expresses the sentence's core action or state.

2. **Find the subject of the main verb.** Ask: *Who or what performs this action?* The subject will be a noun phrase before the verb.

3. **Find the object or complement.** Ask: *What receives the action? What is the state?* The object follows the verb in English.

4. **Mark all clause boundaries.** Every finite verb other than the main verb marks a new clause. Identify what kind of clause each one is (subordinate, relative, conditional).

5. **Remove all subordinate clauses mentally.** Read only the main clause. Restate it in your own words.

6. **Add back the subordinate clauses one at a time.** For each one, identify what it modifies and what logical relationship it expresses (cause, condition, concession, time, purpose, etc.).

7. **Handle nominializations.** If a noun phrase is dense (multiple nominalizations), convert each nominalization back to a verb and reconstruct the implied events.

**Worked example:**

> Because the epistemological assumptions underlying the positivist paradigm, which dominated social science research throughout the early twentieth century, have been increasingly called into question by post-structuralist critiques, contemporary researchers in fields such as sociology and anthropology have begun to adopt interpretive frameworks that prioritize the subjective meaning-making of participants over the objective measurement of variables.

**Step 1 — Find the main verb:** *have begun*

**Step 2 — Find the subject:** *contemporary researchers*

**Step 3 — Find the object:** *to adopt interpretive frameworks*

**Main clause:** *Contemporary researchers have begun to adopt interpretive frameworks.*

**Step 4 — Subordinate clauses:**
- *Because the epistemological assumptions...have been called into question* (causal subordinate clause)
- *underlying the positivist paradigm* (participial phrase modifying *assumptions*)
- *which dominated social science research throughout the early twentieth century* (relative clause modifying *positivist paradigm*)
- *that prioritize the subjective meaning-making of participants over the objective measurement of variables* (relative clause modifying *interpretive frameworks*)

**Step 5 — Restatement:** Because old assumptions are now questioned, researchers are adopting new frameworks that focus on subjective meaning.

### Practice Sentences with Walkthroughs

The following sentences are drawn from subjects covered in DevBook. Each walkthrough demonstrates the Find-the-Verb strategy.

**Sentence 1 (Philosophy):**

> Although existentialist philosophy is often characterized as pessimistic due to its emphasis on the absurdity of human existence, thinkers such as Kierkegaard and Camus maintained that the acknowledgment of absurdity is not an endpoint but a starting point for the authentic construction of meaning.

**Walkthrough:**
- Main verb: *maintained* (past tense, third person plural)
- Subject: *thinkers such as Kierkegaard and Camus*
- Object: *that the acknowledgment...meaning* (a noun clause functioning as object)
- Subordinate clause 1 (concession): *Although existentialist philosophy is often characterized as pessimistic*
- Subordinate clause 2 (causal, embedded in clause 1): *due to its emphasis on the absurdity of human existence*
- Main claim: Existentialist thinkers maintained that acknowledging absurdity is the beginning of authentic meaning-making, not the end.

**Sentence 2 (Psychology):**

> Had the longitudinal study not controlled for socioeconomic status, the observed correlation between childhood trauma and adult health outcomes could have been attributed to a confounding variable rather than to the trauma itself.

**Walkthrough:**
- Main verb: *could have been attributed* (passive, conditional perfect)
- Subject: *the observed correlation between childhood trauma and adult health outcomes*
- This is an inverted conditional: *Had the longitudinal study not controlled for socioeconomic status* = *If the longitudinal study had not controlled for socioeconomic status*
- Main claim: The study controlled for SES, so the correlation is attributable to trauma itself.
- The inversion signals a counterfactual — the condition did not happen, which is why the conclusion is valid.

**Sentence 3 (Epistemology):**

> The claim that knowledge requires justified true belief, a position known as the JTB theory of knowledge, has been subjected to sustained criticism following the publication of Gettier's 1963 paper, which demonstrated that a belief can be both justified and true without constituting knowledge.

**Walkthrough:**
- Main verb: *has been subjected* (present perfect passive)
- Subject: *The claim that knowledge requires justified true belief*
- Appositive: *a position known as the JTB theory of knowledge*
- Subordinate clause 1 (adverbial time): *following the publication of Gettier's 1963 paper*
- Relative clause: *which demonstrated that a belief can be both justified and true without constituting knowledge*
- Main claim: The JTB theory has been criticized since Gettier's paper.

**Sentence 4 (Computer Science):**

> Having demonstrated that the algorithm achieves sublinear time complexity for sparse graphs, the authors proceed to prove that the same algorithm, when applied to dense graphs, converges to the optimal solution within a factor of epsilon of the true optimum, provided that the graph satisfies the triangle inequality.

**Walkthrough:**
- Main verb: *proceed* (present tense, third person plural)
- Subject: *the authors*
- Participial phrase: *Having demonstrated that the algorithm achieves sublinear time complexity for sparse graphs* (completed action, before the main verb)
- Object of *proceed to prove*: *that the same algorithm...converges* (noun clause)
- Embedded subordinate clause (time): *when applied to dense graphs* (reduced adverbial clause = *when it is applied to dense graphs*)
- Embedded subordinate clause (condition): *provided that the graph satisfies the triangle inequality*
- Main claim: The authors prove their algorithm also works on dense graphs under a specific condition.

**Sentence 5 (Ethics):**

> Were it not for the distinction between act-utilitarianism, which evaluates individual actions based on their consequences, and rule-utilitarianism, which evaluates actions based on the consequences of the rules that govern them, utilitarian ethics would lack the conceptual resources to account for the moral significance of rights and duties.

**Walkthrough:**
- Main verb: *would lack* (conditional)
- Subject: *utilitarian ethics*
- Inverted conditional: *Were it not for the distinction...and rule-utilitarianism* (= *If it were not for the distinction...*)
- Relative clause 1 (modifying *act-utilitarianism*): *which evaluates individual actions based on their consequences*
- Relative clause 2 (modifying *rule-utilitarianism*): *which evaluates actions based on the consequences of the rules that govern them*
- Embedded relative in clause 2: *that govern them* (modifying *rules*)
- Main claim: Without the act/rule distinction, utilitarianism cannot account for rights and duties.

## Learning Tips

- **Read academic sentences aloud.** Hearing the clause boundaries helps you feel the structure. Pause at each comma, dash, or parenthesis.
- **Practice the Find-the-Verb strategy daily.** Find a sentence from any DevBook philosophy or psychology module and parse it. Time yourself. Aim for under 30 seconds per sentence.
- **Keep a clause-boundary journal.** For one week, copy one complex sentence per day, draw vertical lines at clause boundaries, and label each clause type.
- **Rewrite nominalizations as verbs.** If a sentence feels opaque, convert each nominalization back to a verb and rewrite the sentence in your own words. Compare your version with the original to see what precision you lost.
- **Use the Glossary.** When parsing fails, check whether an unfamiliar term is obscuring the structure. Sometimes a single unknown word makes the entire sentence unreadable.
- **Do not skim.** Academic sentences carry meaning in their structure. Skimming a complex sentence is like skipping lines in a mathematical proof — you will lose the reasoning.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Appositive** | A noun phrase that renames or defines an adjacent noun, typically set off by commas |
| **Clause** | A group of words containing a subject and a verb |
| **Conditional** | A sentence structure expressing that one event depends on another (if...then) |
| **Dangling participle** | A participial phrase whose implied subject does not match the main clause subject, creating ambiguity |
| **Finite verb** | A verb form that shows tense and agrees with its subject |
| **Inverted conditional** | A conditional that omits *if* and inverts subject and auxiliary (*Had we known...*) |
| **Main clause** | A clause that can stand alone as a sentence |
| **Nominalization** | A noun derived from a verb or adjective (*analysis* from *analyze*) |
| **Non-restrictive clause** | A relative clause that adds extra information without defining the noun, set off by commas |
| **Participial phrase** | A phrase beginning with a present or past participle that functions as an adjective or adverbial |
| **Parenthetical** | An interruption that adds commentary, clarification, or attribution, set off by commas, dashes, or parentheses |
| **Relative clause** | A clause that modifies a noun, beginning with a relative pronoun (which, that, who, etc.) |
| **Restrictive clause** | A relative clause that defines which noun is meant, not set off by commas |
| **Subordinate clause** | A clause that depends on a main clause and cannot stand alone |
| **Subordinating conjunction** | A word that introduces a subordinate clause (because, although, if, etc.) |
